Rusty Bill (@Rusty_Bill) is an independent sports analytics builder who focuses on AI-assisted NFL and NHL prediction/betting models. He shares real results, data tips, and especially AI prompts for building, auditing, backtesting, and improving models (not for asking AI “who wins tonight”). His site is RustyBillSports.com (with NFL/NHL model pages).
He emphasizes: AI is best for building and rigorously testing models rather than generating picks. Key themes across his posts include preventing data leakage/look-ahead bias, proper walk-forward backtesting, realistic EV (including margins and execution), calibration, correlation/risk, version control of rules, out-of-sample validation, and never trusting big backtest edges without diagnosis. Simpler models often beat complex ones; overall accuracy ≠ betting edge; large disagreements with the market can expose model weaknesses rather than true edges.
Thread 1: 8 Prompts for Building, Backtesting & Improving a Betting Model
(From his high-engagement post starting ~Nov 10, 2026)
	1	The Model Architect “Act as a quantitative sports analyst. Help me build a prediction model for [SPORT] and [BET TYPE]. Identify the 10 most predictive variables, where to obtain historical data, how to create a simple baseline, and how to test whether each variable improves predictions. Prioritize measurable data over narratives. Explain which variables could cause data leakage.”
	2	The Data Quality Inspector “Audit my historical [SPORT] dataset before I build a model. Identify missing values, duplicate games, inconsistent player IDs, incorrect dates, outliers, and statistics that weren’t available before kickoff. Create Python code to clean the dataset without silently deleting records. Produce a report of every correction and unresolved issue.”
	3	The Player Prop Projection Builder “Help me build a statistical projection model for [PLAYER PROP]. Use historical game logs, projected playing time, opportunity volume, efficiency, opponent adjustments, and uncertainty. Start with a simple baseline. Write reproducible Python code and produce a projected mean, median, and probability distribution. Never invent missing inputs.”
	4	The Expected Value Calculator “Build a Python calculator that compares my model’s probabilities against sportsbook odds. Convert American odds into implied probabilities, estimate bookmaker margin, and calculate expected value per $1 wager. For player props, derive over/under probabilities from my projected distribution, not just the difference between my projection and the betting line. Include pushes, odds formats, and worked examples.”
	5	The Backtesting Specialist “Design a walk-forward backtest for my [SPORT] betting model. Train only on information available before each game and test on future games. Prevent look-ahead bias, data leakage, and repeated tuning on the test set. Track every qualifying selection, including losses. Report ROI, units, sample size, drawdowns, calibration, and performance by market. Compare results against a simple baseline.”
	6	The Model Improvement Analyst “Analyze my model’s historical predictions against actual results. Break down errors by player, team, opponent, market, projection range, and betting line. Determine whether the model is systematically overestimating or underestimating outcomes. Separate random variance from repeatable weaknesses. Suggest improvements, but require out-of-sample validation before accepting any changes.”
	7	The Betting Performance Tracker “Build a transparent performance tracker for my model using [CSV OR DATABASE]. Record every qualifying play, its timestamp, sportsbook, line, odds, model probability, result, and net units. Calculate win rate, ROI, cumulative profit, and maximum drawdown. Use flat 1-unit risk as the baseline. Never remove losing picks or retroactively change qualification criteria. Flag missing or unverifiable records.”
	8	The Model Automation Engineer “Help me automate my [SPORT] prediction model. Design a workflow that imports updated statistics, validates data freshness, runs projections, compares market lines, and saves timestamped selections before games begin. Keep expensive API calls controlled. Separate administrative refresh actions from public dashboards. Include error handling, caching, usage limits, and alerts for stale data. Do not modify the underlying prediction formulas without approval.”
Closing advice from the thread: Don’t judge by biggest winning weeks. Judge by accuracy on unseen data + transparent record with consistent rules and hundreds of tracked predictions (including losses).
Thread 2: 8 Prompts for Testing, Auditing & Improving Models
(Focused on finding real edge)
	1	The Data Leakage Detective “Act as a quantitative sports data auditor. Review my [SPORT] prediction model and dataset for information that wouldn’t have been available when each prediction was made. Check injury reports, closing odds, player statistics, updated projections, and timestamps. Identify potential look-ahead bias and write Python tests that prevent future data from entering historical predictions. Do not modify my model until I approve the findings.”
	2	The Backtesting Auditor “Act as a quantitative research analyst specializing in sports betting. Audit my model’s historical results for selection bias, overfitting, and misleading profitability. Check whether qualification rules were established before games, whether losing selections were included, and whether historical odds were actually available. Separate genuine pregame predictions from retrospectively selected plays. Calculate ROI, net units, win rate, sample size, and maximum drawdown. Show me which results are independently reproducible.”
	3	The Probability Calibration Expert “Act as a sports modeling statistician. Compare my model’s predicted probabilities against actual outcomes. Group predictions into probability ranges (50–55%, 55–60%, 60–65%, etc.) and calculate the observed win rate for each range. Generate a reliability diagram, Brier score, and log loss. Identify where my model is overconfident or underconfident. Use out-of-sample predictions only, account for small sample sizes, and recommend calibration methods without changing the original model.”
	4	The Expected Value Inspector “Act as a quantitative betting analyst. Review my model’s predicted probabilities and historical sportsbook odds. Calculate expected value per $1 risked using the actual prices recorded before each game. Account for bookmaker margin, exchange commissions, pushes, and unavailable or stale lines. Compare performance using the best available sportsbook price versus a consistent benchmark book. Identify which apparent edges disappear when realistic execution costs are included.”
	5	The Model Bias Hunter “Act as a statistical model validation specialist. Analyze my [SPORT] projections against actual results. Break down prediction errors by player, team, opponent, market, betting direction, projected edge, and time period. Determine whether the model consistently overestimates or underestimates certain outcomes. Separate random variation from repeatable errors using confidence intervals and adequate sample sizes. Recommend improvements, but require testing on unseen data before accepting any changes.”
	6	The Correlation Analyzer “Act as a sports betting risk analyst. Review my historical qualifying bets and identify selections that share underlying risk. Examples include receiving yards and receptions for the same player, multiple unders from one game, and props influenced by the same quarterback or game script. Estimate correlations using historical outcomes where sufficient data exists. Calculate portfolio-level variance, drawdown, and effective exposure. Explain how correlated bets can make a profitable record appear more statistically convincing than it really is.”
	7	The Model Version Tracker “Act as an independent model-performance auditor. Review the history of my prediction formulas, qualification thresholds, model versions, and saved selections. Identify exactly when each rule became active. Separate prospective selections made under rules established before kickoff from historical plays qualified using rules adopted later. Create a reproducible performance report for each version. Never overwrite historical predictions or retroactively improve the reported track record.”
	8	The Performance Stress Tester “Act as a quantitative risk researcher. Stress-test my [SPORT] betting model using its complete historical selection record. Analyze performance under losing streaks, changing market conditions, realistic line availability, and different sample periods. Use bootstrap simulations that account for correlated bets. Estimate uncertainty around ROI, maximum drawdown, and the probability that observed profitability could be due to variance. Compare against an appropriate baseline. Tell me what evidence would be needed before concluding the model has a durable edge.”
Closing lesson: A profitable backtest ≠ durable edge. Predictions must survive new data, realistic prices, changing conditions, and full loss inclusion.
Additional Standalone Prompts He Shares & Uses
	•	Large-edge diagnostic (highly recommended by him; credit often to @PlusEVAnalytics, with his modification): “find the 10 largest back-test edges and diagnose why the model has their win probability so high. If any bugs or model weaknesses are found, fix them. Repeat until you are confident that the 10 largest edges are all legitimate.” His tip: Have AI propose fixes for your approval, then validate on unseen data to avoid overfitting the backtest.
	•	Independent quantitative model auditor (20 largest edges) (one he says he uses across all his projects): “Act as an independent quantitative model auditor. Find the 20 largest projected betting edges in my historical predictions, using only odds and projections saved before each game. For each one:
	1	Show the projected probability, sportsbook odds, implied probability, and calculated edge.
	2	Identify the inputs responsible for the unusually high confidence.
	3	Check for missing data, stale odds, incorrect player mappings, and calculation errors.
	4	Compare the prediction with the actual result, but don’t assume a winning bet proves the projection was correct.
	5	Look for patterns across the 20 selections that could indicate systematic bias.
	6	Flag anything suspicious and explain what evidence would be needed to verify it. Rank the findings by severity. Do not modify my model, change historical predictions, or optimize anything based on past results. Recommend fixes separately and explain how to test them on unseen data.”
	•	Market disagreement analyzer (from NFL passing yards example): “Analyze my historical predictions against the corresponding sportsbook lines. Group every prediction by how far it differed from the market. For each group, calculate the number of bets, predicted win probability, actual win rate, and ROI using the original odds. Identify where larger disagreements are associated with worse results. Use only pregame predictions and clearly flag small samples. Do not change any model parameters.” Lesson he highlights: Big disagreement with Vegas can reveal model weaknesses, not edges. Overall calibration can look fine while betting decisions fail.
Extra Tips & Philosophy He Shares
	•	Test by removing groups of variables one at a time and re-running out-of-sample backtests—more data/complexity often adds nothing.
	•	Always use pregame-saved odds/projections only. Track everything (including losses) with frozen rules.
	•	Focus on EV after realistic costs, calibration (Brier, reliability diagrams), correlations, and stress tests (bootstrap with correlation).
	•	Transparent public tracking > cherry-picked screenshots.
	•	He builds and publicly tracks his own NFL (player props focused) and NHL models on his site; results pages emphasize research models, frozen pregame decisions, 1-unit grading, etc.
